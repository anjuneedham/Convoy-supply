## GATE — read before spending anything on brand assets

**Convoy Supply Ltd.** is a real company: a building-envelope and building-materials wholesale distributor, founded **1972**, headquartered in Surrey, British Columbia, operating across Canada, the Pacific Northwest US, and Maryland, at `convoy-supply.com` **[V2]**.

Before any money goes to a logo, domain, packaging, or paid ads:

1. **USPTO TESS search** in the relevant Nice classes — at minimum **Class 12** (vehicle accessories), **Class 18** (bags and luggage), **Class 35** (retail services)
2. **State-level** business name search in the state of formation
3. **Domain and social handle** availability
4. If anything is ambiguous, **30 minutes with a trademark attorney.** This is the cheapest risk reduction in the entire plan.

**Reduce collision risk by construction:**
- Always **one word, no hyphen: ConvoySupply.** Never "Convoy Supply." The hyphenated/spaced form is theirs.
- **Never** enter building materials, roofing, insulation, siding, or contractor supply under this name
- Do not use a wordmark, color scheme, or logo form resembling theirs
- Consider a descriptive lockup — **"ConvoySupply — Cab Gear"** — to widen the distinction in any category where the mark appears

Recorded as **DEC-001** in `execution/decisions.md`. If clearance fails, the brand renames and only this document, the theme's color tokens, and the logo need to change — which is precisely why the name is not baked into product names.

## Positioning

### Statement

> For professional drivers who live out of their cab, **ConvoySupply** makes purpose-built storage and organization gear that turns a truck's dead space into a system. Unlike generic car organizers and unbranded marketplace listings, everything we make is designed around the actual dimensions and daily reality of a Class 8 sleeper.

### The category we are creating

Not "truck accessories." That is a parts-counter category and it puts us next to chrome bumpers and air horns.

**Cab systems.** The cab is a workspace and a living space, and it can be set up well or badly.

Framing matters commercially: accessories compete on price, systems compete on completeness. A system justifies a bundle. A bundle raises AOV. AOV is what survives a high CPA.

### Position on the map

```
                        PURPOSE-BUILT FOR TRUCKS
                                  ▲
                                  │
        Installed cab shelving    │    ◄── ConvoySupply
        (expensive, permanent)    │        (no install, system-designed)
                                  │
   COMMITTED ◄───────────────────┼───────────────────► NO INSTALL
                                  │
        Truck stop impulse buys   │    Amazon generic organizers
                                  │    (car products, truck keywords)
                                  ▼
                            GENERIC
```

We occupy **purpose-built + no installation**, which is empty, and it is empty for no good reason.

## Brand promise

> **Everything in its place. Nothing in your way.**

What we commit to, and will be held to:

1. **Built for the actual space.** Real dimensions, named cab compatibility, tested in a real truck.
2. **No installation, ever.** Straps, hangs, sits. Nothing drills or bolts. *(This is a product constraint, not just a message — it keeps us buyable by company drivers whose carriers prohibit modifications.)*
3. **Honest specs.** Fabric denier, webbing width, capacity, weight, dimensions. Published.
4. **Honest marketing.** No fake reviews. No fake countdowns. No invented scarcity. No guarantees we can't fund.
5. **A real person answers.** Marketplace messages and email, quickly.

**What we do NOT promise:** the lowest price, the fastest shipping, or a lifetime warranty. We are not going to beat Prime and we are not going to pretend to.

## Tagline options

**Recommended primary:**

> **Squared away.**

Two words. It is trucking and military vernacular for *organized, correct, in order* — which means it signals insider knowledge to the audience and reads as plain English to everyone else. It describes the outcome, not the product. Short enough for a logo lockup.

**Alternates:**

| Tagline | Use case | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Gear for the space you live in** | Homepage hero subhead | Most explanatory; best where the brand is unfamiliar |
| **Built for the cab, not the catalog** | Comparison content, About | Directly positions against generics |
| **Your cab, sorted.** | Short-form, social | Lighter tone |
| **Made for 60 square feet** | Story-led content | Specific, memorable, needs context |
| **Nothing loose. Nothing lost.** | Product/safety angle | Benefit-led |

**Avoid:** anything with "king of the road," "hero," "warrior," or "knights of the highway." The avatar research is explicit that this register reads as patronizing **[H]**.

## Tone of voice

**Direct. Specific. Competent. Never salesy.**

Write like a good supplier talking to a professional — someone who knows the job, respects their time, and does not oversell.

### Rules

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Short sentences. Concrete nouns. | Adjective stacks ("premium ultra-durable heavy-duty") |
| Use real numbers — 1680D, 22", 2.2 lb | Vague claims — "super strong," "huge capacity" |
| Name the trucks | "Fits most vehicles" |
| Say "we don't know" when true | Bluff |
| Admit tradeoffs | Pretend there are none |
| "Professional driver" or "driver" | "Trucker" in formal copy (fine in casual/social) |
| Plain words | Marketing words: *elevate, unlock, game-changer, revolutionary, seamlessly* |

### Voice test

Before publishing, read it aloud and ask: **would a driver with 19 years behind the wheel think this was written by someone who has sat in a sleeper?**

If it could equally sell a phone case, rewrite it.

### Calibration examples

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Elevate your cab with our premium organizational solution!" | "Your passenger seat is not a shelf. This is." |
| "Super durable heavy-duty construction" | "1680D ballistic nylon. Box-stitched at every load point." |
| "Fits most trucks!" | "Fits Cascadia, 579, T680, VNL, LT. Dimensions below — measure if you're unsure." |
| "Limited time offer, act now!" | "$54.99. Free shipping over $75." |
| "Customers love it!" | "We launched this month. No reviews yet — yours would be the first." |

That last row is the whole brand in one line.

## Visual identity

### Color

Utilitarian, industrial, legible at small sizes in a Facebook feed.

| Token | Hex | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `--cs-asphalt` | `#1C1F21` | Primary dark — backgrounds, headers |
| `--cs-steel` | `#3A4247` | Secondary dark — cards, borders |
| `--cs-hiviz` | `#E8B23A` | **Accent** — CTAs, highlights. Safety-amber, not a bright yellow |
| `--cs-concrete` | `#EDEBE7` | Light background — warm, not clinical white |
| `--cs-paper` | `#FAF9F7` | Lightest surface |
| `--cs-line` | `#C9C5BE` | Rules, dividers |
| `--cs-ink` | `#14171A` | Body text on light |
| `--cs-mute` | `#6B7075` | Secondary text |
| `--cs-good` | `#3F7D5C` | In stock, confirmations |
| `--cs-warn` | `#B3502B` | Low stock (only when true) |

**Why hi-viz amber:** it is borrowed from the safety-equipment vocabulary the audience sees every day — vests, placards, warning triangles. It reads as *work*, not as *retail*. It also passes contrast against asphalt and concrete, which a bright red would not at small sizes.

**Deliberately avoided:** chrome and gradients (dated trucking cliché), red-white-and-blue (patriotic trucking is crowded and politically loaded), pastels, neon.

### Typography

| Role | Typeface | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Headlines | **Barlow Condensed** (600/700), uppercase for H1/H2 | Condensed grotesque with a transport-signage lineage. Free on Google Fonts. |
| Body | **Inter** (400/500/600) | Highly legible at small sizes, excellent on mobile, free |
| Specs / numbers | **Roboto Mono** (400) | Monospace signals technical data and makes spec tables scan |

Three families, all free, all fast. Fallback stack: `system-ui, -apple-system, Segoe UI, sans-serif`.

### Layout principles

- **Generous whitespace.** The whole brand is about uncluttered space — a cluttered site contradicts the product.
- **Hard edges.** 2px radius maximum. Rounded corners read consumer-soft.
- **Left-aligned text.** Centered paragraphs read as marketing.
- **Data in tables.** Specs get monospace tables, not prose.
- **Numbered sections** where it aids scanning. Manual-like, not brochure-like.

## Photography and image style

This is the single biggest lever we have. Competitor photography is uniformly poor — white background, no context, no cab **[V4]**. Fixing that is cheap and it is most of the differentiation.

### Rules

1. **Shoot in a real cab.** Real dashboard, real wear, real light. A studio shot of the product on white is a competitor's photo.
2. **Natural light, overcast or golden hour.** Never flash. Never a rendered studio look.
3. **Show wear.** A scuffed steering wheel and a worn seat are credibility, not flaws.
4. **Include the human in fragments** — a hand reaching, a forearm, a boot. Rarely a full face. It puts the viewer in the seat.
5. **Before/after must be honest.** Same cab, same angle, same light, same lens. The "before" is genuinely messy, not staged-messy.
6. **No lens flares, no HDR, no heavy grade.** Slight desaturation, slightly lifted blacks. Documentary, not automotive-advertising.
7. **Chrome-at-sunset is banned.** It is the cliché the audience is most tired of.

### AI-generated imagery — the honesty policy

We will use AI images. We will use them **only** where they cannot mislead about the product:

**Permitted:** backgrounds, lifestyle context without the product, problem-state imagery, infographic bases, texture and pattern work.

**Never:** AI renderings of our actual products presented as product photography. Never AI "customer photos." Never AI images implying a real person used the product.

**Product images must be photographs of the real product.** Non-negotiable — it is the line between marketing and deception, and this audience will detect the difference and say so publicly.

Prompts that respect this line live in `prompts/image-generation-prompts.md`.

## Trust strategy

We are unknown, have no reviews, and are asking for a card. Trust is built through **verifiable specifics**, not through trust badges.

| Lever | Implementation |
|---|---|
| **Radical spec honesty** | Every measurable published. Including where we are not best in class. |
| **No fake reviews — and say so** | "We launched this month. No reviews yet. Yours would be the first." This converts the weakness into evidence of honesty. |
| **Real policies, plainly written** | Returns, shipping, privacy. No legalese wall. |
| **A named human** | A founder note with a real first name. Not "the ConvoySupply Team." |
| **Fast replies** | Response time is the trust signal on Marketplace. Target under 1 hour in waking hours. |
| **Honest shipping times** | Real transit estimates. Never "fast shipping!" |
| **Acknowledge the Amazon question** | Answer it in the FAQ before they ask it. Ducking it costs more than answering it. |
| **Secure checkout, shown not claimed** | Shopify Payments badges, no invented "256-bit military-grade" language |

**Explicitly banned across the entire brand:** fake countdown timers · fake stock counters · "23 people are viewing" · fabricated reviews · fabricated press logos · unfunded lifetime guarantees · "was $199, now $54.99" where $199 was never charged.

Compare-at pricing is used **only** where the higher price was genuinely offered — which, at launch, means **only on bundles**, against the true sum of individual prices.

## Product naming strategy

**Functional, trade-register, no puns.**

Pattern: `CS-0X` + `[Location or Function]` + `[Object type]`

| SKU | Name | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| CS-01 | **Cab Command Organizer** | Where it goes + what it is |
| CS-02 | **Bunk Shelf Net** | Where it goes + what it is |
| CS-03 | **Manifest Folio** | Trade vocabulary + what it is |
| CS-04 | **Haul Bag** | Plain, unpretentious |
| CS-05 | **Dash Power Rail** | Where it goes + what it is |

**Why SKU codes are used publicly:** they signal a catalog maintained by people who take inventory seriously, they make Marketplace listings and support conversations unambiguous, and they make the range feel like a system rather than five unrelated items.

**Avoided:** aspirational names (Nomad, Voyager, Pioneer), aggressive names (Beast, Titan, Warrior), and puns. All three fail the voice test.

**Bundle naming:** outcome-led, since bundles are sold on the result.
- **The Cab Reset Kit** — CS-01 + CS-02 + CS-03
- **The Full Rig Kit** — all four soft goods

## Collection naming

Organized **by where the problem is**, not by product type. A driver thinks in zones, not categories.

| Collection | Contains | Slug |
|---|---|---|
| **Front of Cab** | CS-01, CS-03, (CS-05) | `/collections/front-of-cab` |
| **Sleeper & Bunk** | CS-02, CS-04 | `/collections/sleeper-and-bunk` |
| **Kits** | Both bundles | `/collections/kits` |
| **Everything** | All | `/collections/all` |

At four SKUs this is arguably over-structured. It is built this way because the structure has to hold at fifteen SKUs without a rebuild, and because it teaches the customer to think in zones — which is what makes the bundle feel necessary.

## Packaging direction

Low priority at launch; capture the intent now.

- **Plain kraft mailer or box.** No printed retail packaging — at our volumes it is a waste of margin and it is landfill the moment it arrives.
- **One printed insert card**, 4×6, uncoated stock:
  - Front: wordmark + "Squared away."
  - Back: a genuine thank-you in the brand voice, the returns process in one sentence, a support email, and a single honest ask — *"If this works for you, a review would help more than you'd think. If it doesn't, email me first."*
- **No discount code on the insert** at launch. We do not yet know our repeat rate and cannot afford to discount blind.
- **Woven label on soft goods** — wordmark only, small, on an inside seam. Visible to the owner, not to the feed.

## Brand expansion boundary

**Inside the boundary:** anything solving storage, organization, or workspace problems in a vehicle someone works out of. Trucks first, then work vans, then delivery vehicles.

**Outside:** apparel, chrome, performance parts, electronics (except gated CS-05), anything consumable, and **anything in construction or building materials — that boundary is legal, not strategic (see the gate above).**

The test: *would a driver be surprised to see ConvoySupply sell this?* Surprise means we have drifted.
