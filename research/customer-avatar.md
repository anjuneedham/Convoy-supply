### Two sub-segments worth separating

| | **Company driver** | **Owner-operator** |
|---|---|---|
| Share | ~84% of drivers **[V3]** | ~16% **[V3]** |
| Truck | Not theirs | Theirs |
| Spending posture | Modest, personal-comfort framed | Larger, "it's my business" framed |
| Willingness to modify cab | Limited by carrier policy | Unrestricted |
| **Our read** | Higher volume, lower AOV | Lower volume, higher AOV, **stronger buyer** |

**[H] Owner-operators are the better first target.** They own the space, they treat the truck as a business asset, and non-attached soft goods sidestep any carrier modification policy. Company drivers are the volume follow-on.

Non-attached matters more than it sounds: everything in our catalog straps, hangs, or sits. Nothing drills, bolts, or wires (except gated CS-05). That makes the entire range purchasable by a company driver without asking anyone's permission.

## Lifestyle

- **Lives in the truck 200+ nights a year [H].** The sleeper is not a vehicle feature; it is an apartment.
- **Time is fragmented.** Fourteen-hour clock, HOS rules, loading docks, detention. Free time arrives in unpredictable 20-minute blocks.
- **Home is somewhere else.** Deliveries to a home address may sit unopened for three weeks. **This single fact shapes our entire shipping policy.**
- **Meals are truck stops, coolers, and microwaves.**
- **Social life is a phone.** Family, driver friends, and Facebook Groups.
- **Physical job, aging body.** Reaching, twisting, and climbing all cost something at 54.

## The problem, in their words **[H]**

Not quoted from anyone. This is our best reconstruction of the internal monologue, and testing ad copy against it is one way we find out whether we got it right.

> *"I know it's in here somewhere."*
> *"Everything ends up on the passenger seat."*
> *"I've got a $180,000 truck and nowhere to put a pair of gloves."*
> *"Every time I brake hard, half of it's on the floor."*
> *"It's embarrassing when somebody gets in."*

That last line is the important one. **The emotional core is not disorganization. It is living somewhere that feels out of your control.**

## Current alternatives

| Alternative | Why it's chosen | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing — a pile on the passenger seat | Free, zero effort | It is the problem |
| Plastic totes and milk crates | Cheap, available | Slide, no lid, ugly, waste space |
| Consumer car seat-back organizers | On Amazon, $15 | Sized for a minivan; straps don't reach; collapse under weight |
| Grocery and plastic bags | Free | Tear, rustle, look terrible |
| Aftermarket cab shelving | Purpose-built | Expensive, needs installation, often carrier-prohibited |
| Truck stop impulse buys | Immediate | Overpriced, poor quality, unsystematic |

**[H] The whitespace is precise:** purpose-built for a Class 8 cab, requires no installation, costs under $60. Consumer organizers are too small; cab shelving is too committed. Nothing sits comfortably between.

## Frustrations (beyond the product problem)

Relevant because they shape tone of voice more than they shape the product:

1. **Being condescended to.** Marketing that treats drivers as unsophisticated. Deeply resented **[H]**.
2. **Being sold junk at truck stops** at captive-audience prices **[H]**.
3. **Products "for trucks" that are obviously for pickups** **[H]**.
4. **Nobody understanding the job** — including the people selling to them **[H]**.

**Implication for us, and it is the most important line in this document:** the fastest way to lose this customer is to sound like a marketer. The fastest way to earn them is to demonstrate we know what a bunk wall is.

## Desired outcome

**Functional [H]:** everything has a place; nothing moves under braking; paperwork produced in seconds; gets the passenger seat back.

**Emotional [H]:** *"My cab is squared away."* Control, order, and a small amount of pride in a space nobody else sees.

We sell the functional outcome and let the emotional one sit underneath. Leading with emotion in this market reads as manipulation.

## Price sensitivity **[H]**

Not cheap, but **highly value-alert.** This buyer will pay above the Amazon generic for something demonstrably built for the job, and will punish a price that isn't justified.

- **Under $30** — low friction, near-impulse (CS-02, CS-03)
- **$30–$60** — considered, needs a reason (CS-01, CS-04)
- **$60–$130** — needs a bundle frame to feel rational
- **Over $150** — **[H]** requires trust we have not yet earned

Free shipping threshold at **$75** is deliberate: it sits just below the Cab Reset Kit at $89, pulling single-item buyers toward the bundle.

### Facebook behavior specifically **[H]**
- Sessions are short and frequent — parking lots, docks, waiting on a load
- Engages with content that **names the job accurately**
- Deeply suspicious of ads; will screenshot and mock bad ones in Groups
- Responds to peer content far more than brand content
- **Comments before clicking.** Comment volume will likely lead click volume — worth knowing so we don't misread early ad data.

### Marketplace behavior **[H]**
- Comfortable with the "is this available?" ritual
- Expects a fast reply and judges the seller by response speed
- Negotiation is assumed
- Will check the seller's profile for legitimacy — **our Marketplace presence must look like a real business**

## Content they engage with **[H]**

**Works:**
- Real photos of real cabs — including messy ones
- "Show me your setup" peer content
- Practical lists that respect their time
- Honest cost breakdowns
- Humor about the job, **from inside the job**
- Anything acknowledging how hard parking is

**Fails:**
- Stock photography of clean trucks at sunset
- Corporate voice
- Motivational "hero of the highway" content — widely read as patronizing
- Hype, fake urgency, countdown timers
- Anyone who uses "trucker" wrong or calls the sleeper a "bed area"

## Why they would trust ConvoySupply **[H]**

1. **We describe the problem accurately.** Recognition precedes trust. If the ad copy sounds like it was written by someone who has seen a sleeper cab, we are already ahead of most competitors.
2. **Specs are published.** Denier, webbing width, dimensions, weight.
3. **No fake reviews.** We say "join the first customers" rather than inventing testimonials — and the honesty is itself a differentiator in a category full of obviously-purchased reviews.
4. **Real returns policy, plainly worded.**
5. **Shipping reality is acknowledged** — including that a driver may not be home.
6. **Fast, human replies** on Marketplace and email.

## Why they would NOT buy **[H]**

Ranked most to least likely:

1. **Amazon.** Same-ish product, two-day delivery, a brand they already trust with a card on file. This is the real competitor and it is a good one.
2. **"Never heard of them."** No reviews, no social proof, unknown domain.
3. **Fit uncertainty.** If the PDP does not resolve "will this fit my truck," they leave.
4. **Delivery logistics.** No confidence a package will reach them.
5. **Price without justification.**
6. **Tone failure.** One patronizing line and they are gone, permanently.
7. **No urgency.** The pile on the passenger seat has been there two years. It can stay another month.

**#7 is underrated and mostly unaddressed by our current plan.** This is a *latent* problem, not an *acute* one. It causes no pain on any given day. The marketing job is not persuading them the product is good — it is making a tolerable annoyance feel worth solving today. That is what the before/after creative is actually for: not to prove the product works, but to make the current state suddenly visible.
